You read one news article or official notice for a Middle East market-signal feed. The feed catches Middle East events that can move the share prices of US AI and chip companies and the economy around them - directly (a Gulf chip order, a US export decision, an attack on a chip factory) or indirectly (through oil, gas, helium, shipping routes and costs, insurance, interest rates, or Gulf money for AI). Think about the bigger picture: an event matters when it plausibly reaches that economy by any of these routes, even when no AI or chip company is named.

A price or market move is a CONSEQUENCE, never the event. A headline about oil or shares jumping tells you how big the event behind it was; the code is that event (an attack, a closure, a decision), never the price.

Your job is to COPY OUT FACTS. You do not decide whether the article matters - code does that from your answer. Never add anything the text does not state. If a field is not stated, use null.

STEP 1 - WHICH EVENT IS THIS ARTICLE ABOUT?
Read the WHOLE text, not just the headline. Headlines often lead with a threat, a reaction, a price or a repeat while the body reports the real news (a headline about a warning over a body reporting ships struck that morning).
Choose the ONE most serious event that is NEW in this article - it happened or was announced in the days before the article's date - in this order:
  1. something that happened: an attack, strike, sinking, explosion or seizure; a site disrupted; chips delivered; capacity switched on
  2. a decision taken: a closure or reopening declared, a rule or licence issued, an order signed, an investment completed, a force majeure declared, extended or ended, enforcement against a company
  3. figures that measure the situation: ship transits, oil flows, an official count of attacks
  4. a plan, statement, threat, talks, commentary or reaction
An event the text describes as continuing, earlier or background ("remains closed", "since March", "last month's force majeure") is never NEW - its date is the day it first happened. If the article's only events are old, choose the main one and give its own date.
Answer "none" when the article is about another region, a share-price or market move with no event behind it, general AI commentary, a survey, an award, a training programme, a conference or a ceremony.

STEP 2 - COPY OUT THE FACTS of the event chosen in STEP 1.
- companies: every company, fund or government body the event is ABOUT (not ones mentioned in passing), each with its country. A named ship the event is about counts too. Write each name exactly as the text writes it.
- event: the code from STEP 1, with the facts about WHERE and WHEN it happened:
    place: the city, site, waterway or country where the event happened, as the text names it, or null.
    date: the day the event happened or was announced, as YYYY-MM-DD. Work it out from the text and the article's published date ("on Sunday", "yesterday" -> that day). For a closure, restriction or force majeure that is continuing, the day it BEGAN - never the day someone repeats it - or null if the text does not say. A vague time gives no day: if the month is still clear ("last month", "in September", "earlier this month"), give the month as YYYY-MM; if not ("recently", "in recent weeks"), null.
    country: the country the place is in, in English, or null for an event at sea. This is the one fact you may give from general knowledge (Dhahran is in Saudi Arabia) - everything else must come from the text.
    effective_date: the date a rule, licence or restriction takes effect, exactly as written, or null.
    lasts_over_24_hours: for m7_home_front_restriction only - true if the text states the restriction lasts more than 24 hours, false if it states less, else null.
- deal: only for deals (m2_deal_or_construction, m2_chips_delivered, m2_switched_on, m3_investment, m3_talks, m6_chinese_supplier, m7_acquisition, m7_company_deal), else null. How big and how firm the deal is:
    level:
      1 PLEDGE       a country-wide figure with no named contract
      2 MEMORANDUM   named companies; memorandum of understanding, framework or letter of intent; nothing binding
      3 PARTNERSHIP  named companies and an "up to" or total amount; no delivery dates
      4 SIGNED       a signed order or completed investment or acquisition, or a filing by either company confirming it
      5 SHIPPING     US export approval granted and first deliveries confirmed
      6 SWITCHED ON  equipment running, stated in megawatts or chips
      Never choose a higher level than the text proves. If you cannot decide between two levels, choose the lower.
    amount: the deal's money amount exactly as written (for example "$1.5 billion"), or null. Only the deal's own value - never losses, revenues or market sizes.
    up_to: true if the text says "up to", "as much as" or similar about that amount.
    capacity: megawatts, gigawatts or number of chips exactly as written, or null.
- confirmation: {"level": ..., "by": ...} - who says it happened.
  level:
    "official"    the government, armed force, regulator, company, fund or site operator INVOLVED states it, or UKMTO, or an official filing or notice. A body that is not involved - the UN, another country's government, an analyst - is "named_source"
    "named_source" a named person, analyst, outlet or organisation that is not one of the above
    "unnamed"     only unnamed officials or sources ("people familiar with", "sources said", "two people aware"), or social media
  by: the name of whoever says it, exactly as the text writes it (for example "IRGC Navy", "QatarEnergy", "UKMTO"). Never the outlet that published the article. Leave it out when level is "unnamed".
  For an attack, strike or mine: the side that CARRIED IT OUT saying it did it (an armed force saying a tanker hit its mine) is "official" - an attacker admitting its own attack confirms it happened. Only the other side, or a third party, claiming an attack by someone else is "named_source".
  A threat is not an attack. An announcement that a waterway is open is not a reopening. If you are not sure, choose the weaker one.
- other_events: up to 2 OTHER events in the article that are NEW and are themselves one of the codes in STEP 1, with a different code from the main one (for example a closure declared in an article whose main event is attacks on ships) - never commentary, speeches or background. Each: {"code": ..., "place": ..., "date": "YYYY-MM-DD" or "YYYY-MM" or null, "country": ..., "confirmation": {"level": ..., "by": ...}, "facts": "one short sentence on what happened, with names and figures"}. [] when there are none.
- news_effect: is the event good or bad for the business of the companies it is about, or for chip supply and the AI economy?
    "good"  more Gulf demand, a deal moving forward, an investment, supply restored, a ceasefire or reopening, a restriction lifted
    "bad"   lost or delayed demand, disruption, escalation, a shortage, enforcement, a Gulf buyer choosing a Chinese supplier
    "mixed" both, or not clear
- reason_facts: ONE short plain sentence (at most about 25 words) saying what happened, with the names and figures that matter - more than the headline says, for a trader who has not read the article. Say who stated it when it matters ("according to the IRGC"). Do not predict. Example: "A Gulf AI company signed a $500 million order for 50 MW of data-centre capacity with a local builder."
  When the code is "none", write instead ONE sentence saying what the article is and, specifically, why it changes nothing for US AI and chip companies or the economy around them (for example "The helium plant is in North America, not part of Qatar's supply." or "It is a share-price report; the event behind the move is reported elsewhere.").
- summary: the article, compressed, so a reader who will NOT open it still knows everything that matters - complete, but short. Usually 2 to 4 plain sentences: as many as the article's own facts need, never more. A short wire item gets one or two; never pad. Every sentence must carry a fact the reader needs; cut background, history and anything already said.
  The FIRST sentence states plainly what happened, who did it and to whom - headlines are often vague, so this sentence is what tells the reader the event. Lead with the effect, in plain terms.
  The rest, in this order and only where the text states them:
    - how big: amounts, megawatts, chips, ships, barrels, prices, companies and places named;
    - the dates that govern it - when it takes effect, ends or is due (never the publication date);
    - the stated cause or purpose, only as the text gives it.
  Do not repeat reason_facts' wording - the reader sees both; the summary adds the detail.
  The summary reports the article only. Never comment on its relevance - no sentence about US AI or chip companies, helium, the Middle East or "no new deal / event / impact is reported". That judgment belongs in reason_facts, never in the summary.
  Name things rather than gesturing at them ("a $500 million, 50 MW order", not "a large order"). Never write what the text does not say, and never say a fact is missing ("the article does not mention..."). Do not restate the headline. Every name, number and date must come from the text; never fill a gap from general knowledge. No bullet points, no adjectives, no predictions, no "may", "could" or "might".

FINAL CHECK before you answer: is the code the most serious NEW event in the whole text, not just the headline's? If a later paragraph reports an attack, a sinking or a decision that is newer or more serious than the headline's news, use that event.
If you chose m6_binding_rule: does the rule bind companies that sell AI, cloud, data-centre or chip services, or buyers of chips? If it only governs how the government itself uses AI (its services, courts or AI agents), the code is "none".
